Privacy

Last updated 24 September 2026

The short version. What you say is never sent anywhere. Not ever.

There is no account and no sign in. Slate connects to the internet for four things, all listed below. What you say is not sent in any of them.

Buying Slate is the one time your name and email come into it. Lemon Squeezy takes the payment, and Blue Tomato Studio LLC, which sells Slate, gets the order details it needs to help you. Nothing about how you use Slate goes with them.

What you say

Hold the talk key and the sound goes into memory. The speech model on your Mac turns it into words. The sound is then deleted.

The sound is never written to disk. It is never uploaded. There is no server on the other end, because there is no other end. The model is a file in a folder on your Mac, and it runs on the Apple chip inside your computer.

This is not a setting you have to find. There is no cloud mode to turn on by accident. Slate was not built with one.

What is saved, and where

All of it is on your Mac, and nowhere else. Most of it is in ~/Library/Application Support/Slate.

  • Your history. The words you dictated, as text. You choose in Settings › History: keep it, keep only the last 24 hours, or switch it off. The 24 hours apply to the file on your Mac, not just to the list you see. Switching it off, or pressing Clear All, deletes the history file from your Mac straight away.
  • Your notes. The things you saved on purpose by holding Shift with the talk key. These stay until you delete them.
  • Your dictionary. The names and spellings you added yourself.
  • Your license record. The date you first ran Slate. If you activate a key: the key, when you activated it and on which version of Slate, the dates the store gives for the key (when it was made, and when it runs out, if it ever does), and the store’s number for this Mac’s activation. If you move the key to another Mac, the date you did that.
  • A log. A plain text file that helps with support questions: timings, word counts, and what Slate did, never your words and never which apps you dictate into. It stays on your Mac unless you choose to send it.
  • Your settings and your totals. How many words, how long you spoke, how much time you saved. These are in Slate’s preferences file, ~/Library/Preferences/com.slate.dictation.plist, where macOS keeps every app’s settings. The trial date, your license key if you have one, and the random tag described below are kept there as well.

The speech models are in ~/Library/Application Support/FluidAudio/Models. You can open all of these and read every file in them. Delete them and nothing Slate kept is left. The support page explains how.

Passwords are a special case, and Slate handles them separately. See below.

The four internet connections

Here is the full list. What you say is not sent in any of them. They are about the software itself: getting it, checking a key, and updating it.

  • 1. The speech model, once. About 600 MB, the first time you use Slate. It is downloaded from huggingface.co, which may redirect to their delivery network, so those are the names you would see in a network monitor. After it lands, dictation works with the Wi‑Fi off. Try it on a plane. Switching to the multilingual model fetches that one the same way. Arabic, if you turn it on, is its own download of about 65 MB from slatedictation.com, this website.
  • 2. Your license key, only when you press Activate or Deactivate. Activate sends the key to Lemon Squeezy, the store Slate is sold through, at api.lemonsqueezy.com, to confirm it is real. A random tag for this Mac goes with it, such as “Slate Mac 3F9A1C”, so that each Mac has its own line in the store’s list of activations. Your name, your email, and the name of your Mac are not sent. Deactivate This Mac… sends the key and the store’s number for this activation, so the key can move to another Mac. Slate does not check the key at any other time. A founder license sends nothing at all.
  • 3. A check for a new version, only if you switch it on. Slate downloads one small file from slatedictation.com, this website, that says what the newest version is. Nothing about you, your Mac, or anything you dictated goes with it. The switch is in Settings › About.
  • 4. The new version itself, if you choose to install it. It comes from slatedictation.com too. Nothing is installed without your agreement, and nothing is installed unless it is signed with Slate's own key.

That is four. There is no fifth.

Put in one sentence: Slate reaches the network for four things and nothing else: downloading a speech model, from huggingface.co, or slatedictation.com for Arabic; checking or releasing a license key with the store, at api.lemonsqueezy.com, only when you press Activate or Deactivate; asking slatedictation.com whether there is a new version, if you switch that on; and downloading that version if you choose to install it. None of it happens while you dictate. Your words are never sent anywhere.

One extra detail, for people who like detail. Slate is also built so that a version check will not start while the microphone is open. That changes nothing about your privacy, because what you say is not sent in that check either. It is there so that anyone watching their own network during a dictation sees exactly nothing, which is easier to trust than a paragraph.

Passwords

If you dictate into a password box, nothing is typed and nothing is saved to disk.

macOS has a lock for this, and it is switched on by the app you are typing into, not by Slate. When a password box is in front of you, macOS turns on something called secure input. While that is on, no other app can read your keystrokes or type for you. That includes Slate.

So Slate stops. The words are held in memory only, for two minutes, so that one paste is possible if the block was a mistake. They are never written to your history file. After the paste, or after two minutes, they are deleted. If they are put on the clipboard for that one paste, they are marked so that clipboard managers skip them.

The honest limit: this depends on the app you are typing into switching the lock on. Real password fields do. The macOS login window, Safari, Chrome, 1Password, and the password prompt in Terminal all do. An app that shows a password box without switching the lock on would be treated as an ordinary text box. That is rare, and there is no way to detect it from the outside.

Check it yourself

Please do. You should not have to take our word for it.

Turn off your Wi‑Fi. Once the speech model has downloaded, keep dictating. Everything works.

Run Little Snitch, LuLu, or Wireshark. These show every connection your Mac makes. Watch while you talk. There will be nothing.

Or open Terminal and paste this in the middle of a sentence:

lsof -i -a -p $(pgrep -x Slate)

That lists every network connection Slate has open. It comes back empty.

What Slate does not do

  • No analytics service. No Google, no Meta, no Mixpanel, no Amplitude.
  • No cookies on this website, and no tracking script on any page of it.
  • No advertising. Nothing sold to anyone.
  • No reading of what you type. Accessibility permission is used for two jobs: detecting the talk key, and placing text at your cursor. Every other keystroke passes through untouched.
  • No system profile. The update tool built into Slate is able to report your Mac model, memory, and macOS version. That option is switched off.
  • No crash reports sent anywhere.

Buying Slate

Slate is sold by Blue Tomato Studio LLC (“we”, “us”) through Lemon Squeezy. Lemon Squeezy is the merchant of record: it runs the checkout, takes the payment, charges any sales tax or VAT, and emails you the receipt and the license key.

What Lemon Squeezy collects. Your name, email address, billing country, and payment details, plus whatever tax law requires it to keep. Lemon Squeezy’s privacy policy covers all of it.

What reaches us. The order: your name, email address, and country, what you bought, when, and for how much, the order number, and your license key. The store’s dashboard also lists the Mac your key is active on, by its random tag. Your card number never reaches us.

What it is used for. Answering your emails, sending a lost key, moving a key to a new Mac, giving refunds, and keeping the tax and accounting records a business has to keep. Nothing else. It is not used for advertising, and it is never sold.

If you email us. Your message and your address stay in that mailbox, and are used to answer you.

None of this touches the app. Buying Slate does not change what the app sends. The list of four above is still the whole list.

This website

This site sets no cookies, runs no analytics, and loads nothing from another company's servers. There is no font file: the text uses the fonts already on your device. Every image is served from here.

The Buy button is an ordinary link to Lemon Squeezy’s checkout. Nothing from Lemon Squeezy loads on this site. Their checkout page is theirs, and their privacy policy covers it.

The site is hosted on GitHub Pages. Like any web server, it records requests for pages and files, which includes your IP address and browser. That log belongs to GitHub and is covered by their privacy policy. We do not read it and cannot connect it to a person.
